## Best practices: building estimates
- **Structure**: the job holds assemblies by area or trade ("Deck", "Railing", "Stairs"); each assembly holds its items. Put work where a customer would look for it; never one flat list of everything.
- **Measurements stop at the top assembly, not the job**: each top-level assembly (the deck, the stairs, the railing) owns the measurements its work uses (inherit 0 there), and everything inside it inherits from it. Never let an assembly's dimensions inherit up to the job itself: the assembly then carries its measurements wherever it is saved, reused or added, and they all come into the takeoff grouped under that assembly instead of loose on the job. Only a measurement that truly belongs to the whole job (lot size, house footprint) lives on the job.
- **Organize the takeoff into the fewest layers**: every measurement comes into the takeoff on a layer, and each layer is one thing the estimator draws or counts. Group them so there is one layer per drawing, never one per measurement:
  - Everything that comes from one drawing is one group: tracing the deck outline gives its area, perimeter and ledger; one stair gives its treads, width, rise and stringer length; one fence line gives its length, height and posts. They share the same takeoff tool and group (oDimensions[code].takeoff.toolId and takeoff.layerGroupKey), so drawing once fills them all.
  - A measurement calculated from that drawing's measurements (deck depth from area ÷ ledger, joists from the ledger and spacing, beams from the ledger and beam spacing) joins the same group, never a layer of its own.
  - A setting that shapes that drawing (spacing, height, riser height, board width, any measurement with preset options) joins the same group too, as one of its settings.
  - A new layer only for a different thing on the drawing: the railing is its own line, the stairs their own count, the posts their own count when they are placed one by one. Never two groups for the same drawing, and never the same measurement twice in one group.
  - What a drawing cannot give is typed, and it still belongs to that drawing: deck height, post spacing, stair width and riser height are the drawing's inputs. The guided takeoff asks for each input right after its drawing, even when it has a value, so give it a sensible default (or presets) and leave it empty only when there is no sensible default. A contractor standard the estimator never changes per job (beam spacing, joist spacing) gets its value and takeoff: { askInGuide: false } on the dimension (beside its other takeoff settings), so the guide never asks for it.
  - Before you finish, read the takeoff grouping back: each layer should be something a person would draw once. A layer holding a single calculated value or setting, or two layers for the same outline, means it is not organized yet.
- **Quantities come from measurements**: link each item to the assembly's dimensions with a formula (deck area, railing length, post count), never a typed number, so changing a measurement reprices the job. Units convert automatically; never multiply by 12 or divide by 144 yourself.
- **A line measured in length, area or volume never has a blank formula**: a blank formula prices exactly one unit (1 ft of service cable bought as a 500 ft box). Link it to the measurement of its run or surface; when the job has no measurement for it, give it a stated allowance in its unit ("=25" ft of service cable) and say so in the description. Only a count line can stand at one by default.
- **Counts fit the products they go into**: a count never exceeds the capacity of the product chosen to hold it — breakers within the panel's spaces, zones within the controller's, fixtures within the kit or the circuit. When the count does not fit, choose the larger product or lower the count, and never leave the two disagreeing.
- **The item's unit is the kind of measure its formula gives**: a formula that returns deck area makes the line priced per sq ft, a run length per ft, a count per each, a volume per cu ft. Never an area formula on a per-foot line (500 sq ft of membrane priced as 500 ft) or a length on a per-sq-ft line: check the unit every time you set or change a formula, and dimensionalize the product to that unit. They must agree from either side: when the unit is fixed by how the product is sold (a membrane per sq ft) and the measurement you have is a different kind, the formula converts it with the job's own measurements: a run length times its height or width gives area ("rail*railh" for a railing infill priced per sq ft, "deledger*0.5" for a 6 in flashing strip per sq ft), area times a depth gives volume ("slaba*slabh"), a length divided by spacing gives a count ("ceil(fenl/8)+1" posts). Units of the same kind convert by themselves; different kinds never do.
- **A repeated measurement is a dimension**: when two or more items carry the same quantity in a unit other than count (120 sq ft on the boards, the membrane and the install labor; 64 ft on the fascia and the trim) and are not linked to a dimension, that number is one measurement typed into several places. Make it a dimension on the assembly they share (an existing code when one fits, such as deck area or perimeter) and link every one of those items to it with a formula, so it is entered once and a change can never update one line and miss the others. Counts can coincide by chance; lengths, areas and volumes almost never do.
- **Waste lives only in the waste field**: an item's waste (cuts, offcuts, breakage, overage, compaction of loose fill) is its material waste factor (cost_type_material_waste_factor_net, 0.10 = 10%), never in the formula and never in the coverage. The formula is what the work needs ("dcka", never "dcka*1.1" or "dcka*1.05"); the coverage is what one purchase unit covers installed. Waste in the formula also inflates the labor and every line linked to that quantity, and hides it from the person reading the estimate; waste in two places counts it twice. A multiplier in a formula is only ever a density or a geometric factor (2 screws per sq ft, a 6 in depth, 1.5 ft of rebar per sq ft), never extra material.
- **Material and labor are separate lines**: a material line prices the product; an installation line carries hours per unit and a labor type. Every labor line needs a labor type or it prices at $0.
- **Dimensionalize every item tied to a measurement**: the item is bought in its purchase unit (a board, a box, a bag, a sheet) but priced and quantified in the unit of what it covers, with its coverage per purchase unit (how much one board, box or bag covers once installed, after lap; never waste), so the purchase quantity comes out of the coverage.
  - **"Dimensionalize X" means exactly this**, whoever says it ("dimensionalize the ipe", "make it per square foot", "price it by the foot"): convert X to the unit of the measurement its line uses, set its coverage, and reprice it per that unit. Never ask what it means; do it, and show the numbers (purchase price, coverage per purchase unit, price per unit).
  - **Board coverage = length × (face width + gap)**: a 16' 2x6, 1x6 or 5/4x6 (5.5 in face + 1/8 in gap = 5.625 in = 0.46875 ft) covers 16 × 0.46875 = 7.5 sq ft, so its price per sq ft is the board price ÷ 7.5; a 16' 1x4 (3.5 in + 1/8 in = 3.625 in = 0.302 ft) covers 4.83 sq ft. Never round the width to 0.5 ft: that prices every board about 6% low. Take the length and nominal size from the product name, use the actual face width (nominal minus 1/2 in for 4 to 6 in boards), and when only a per-linear-foot price is given, price per sq ft = price per ft ÷ face-plus-gap width in feet.
  - **Choose that unit by working backwards from the takeoff**: ask how the work would be measured on a drawing, with which takeoff tool, and dimensionalize to what that tool gives:
    - An area traced as a closed shape (area tool) → area: deck surface, floor finish, sheathing, roofing, a wall's finish or paint (wall length × height).
    - A line drawn along a run (line tool: total, inside or outside length) → length: baseboard and trim (inside length), fascia, skirting and gutter (outside length), railing, fence, ledger.
    - Members laid across a traced span (span lines: count and total length of joists, beams, boards) → length (or count) of the member: joists, beams, studs.
    - Points placed one by one (count tool) → count: posts, footings, fixtures, doors, lights.
    - A traced area or count with a depth or size (slab area × thickness, footing count × footing size) → volume: concrete, gravel, mulch, fill.
  - The same work can be measured more than one way; pick the one its takeoff actually uses: wall framing is a line (wall length, with studs from the length and spacing) or an area (wall area) depending on how the walls are drawn, while wall finishing is always area.
  - **Coverage items** (the product itself covers the measurement: boards, sheets, rolls, bags of fill): the quantity formula is just the matching measurement (deck area, wall length, footing volume), with no conversion factor in it: "dcka", never "dcka*2.35" to turn area into board feet. The conversion lives in the item's coverage, once.
  - **Packages are dimensionalized to the measurement; the formula outputs the measurement**: a bag, box, pail, bucket, roll, tube, can or sheet is set to the unit its formula measures, with the package as its purchase unit and the package's yield as its coverage: a 60 lb concrete bag becomes volume (about 0.45 cu ft), a pail of joint compound the board area it finishes, a roll of tape or a caulk tube the length it runs. The formula is the footing volume, the wall area, the run length: never a package count ("post*5" bags, "ceil(area/450)" pails), never a yield divided into it, and never left blank as a "fixed quantity". This is what lets one line offer packages of different sizes (50, 60 or 80 lb bags; 1 or 5 gal pails) as options with one formula, and keeps the quantity right when the measurement changes. Only tools and rentals (trowels, brushes, a mixer) are a fixed quantity.
  - **coverageApplied says which price you are giving, and it must be true when the cost is per unit**: a dimensionalized option, add-on or item carries both the purchase price (per board, bag, box: what the supplier charges) and the cost per unit of measure (per sq ft, ft, cu ft). Cost per unit = purchase price ÷ coverage. Whenever the cost or rate you set is already per unit of measure (already divided by the coverage), set coverageApplied: true, so nothing divides it again. Set it false (or leave it out) only when the cost you give is the purchase price per purchase unit, so the coverage division is still to be done. Never both: a per-board price marked as applied charges the board price per sq ft (a $39.81 16' board priced at $39.81 per sq ft, 7.5× too much), and a per-sq-ft cost not marked applied gets divided again (7.5× too little). Check it back: purchase price = cost per unit × coverage, and the option's per-unit cost must match the line's when it is selected.
  - **Units: the line's unit is convertible, the purchase unit is how it is bought**: every item's unit of measure is 'count' or a convertible measure (the keys of c.conversionTables: a length such as in, ft, yd, m; an area such as ft2, yd2, m2, sqr; a volume such as ft3, yd3, m3; less often a weight such as lbs, kg, ton), so formulas, takeoff measurements and unit conversion work on it. The purchase unit says how it is bought: something sold one by one and priced per one (a toilet, a faucet, a door, a single board or sheet) is purchase unit 'count'; anything sold in a package is bought by that package, a unit that is NOT convertible ('bag', 'box', 'package', 'pail', 'bucket', 'roll', 'bundle', 'pallet', 'gallon', 'pair', 'set'). So a properly dimensionalized material always has a purchase unit different from its unit of measure (a bag of concrete: unit ft3, purchase unit bag, coverage 0.45; a 2-pack of knobs: unit count, purchase unit package, coverage 2; a 16' deck board: unit ft2, purchase unit count, coverage 8). Only a one-by-one item has both as 'count' (coverage 1). The same unit on both sides of a non-count item, or a convertible purchase unit, means it was never dimensionalized.
  - **Loose bulk materials are a volume, bought by the cubic yard once the job is bulk-sized**: concrete, base stone, sand, soil and mulch are measured as a volume (area × depth, a footing volume) and their coverage is per purchase unit of that volume (27 cu ft per cubic yard, 0.45 cu ft per 60 lb bag). Never an area line over a "sq ft per yard" coverage: that hides the depth in the coverage, where nobody can read or change it. Bags are for small pours and patches; once the quantity reaches about a cubic yard (a slab, a driveway, a pad beyond a few sq ft) buy truck-delivered ready-mix or bulk stone by the yard, never hundreds of bags.
  - **Digging is sized by the hole, not by what fills it**: an excavation line measures the volume dug (the excavation volume, or the dug area × the dig depth), which is always larger than the concrete, gravel or base placed in it: the hole is wider than a footing for working room and forms, and deeper by any base under it. Never size an excavation by the footing, slab or pour volume, and never give the dig and the pour the same measurement.
  - **Every multi-pack is reduced to one unit**: anything not sold one by one is priced per single piece, with the pack as the purchase unit and the pieces in it as the coverage, whatever it is: screws, but also door knobs in a 2-pack, light switches or outlets in a 10-pack, cabinet pulls in a 25-pack. Items sold one by one (a toilet, a faucet, a light fixture, a door) are already one unit. The same product comes in different purchase formats (single, 2-pack, 4-pack, contractor pack), and only a price per ONE unit compares them apples to apples, lets them be options side by side, and buys the right number of packs from the count the job needs. A pack priced as one piece buys a pack for every knob and makes the cheaper per-piece product look dearer. Read the pack size from the name ("2-Pack", "Pack of 4", "(10 ct.)"); when it is not stated, judge it from the price against a typical single piece, and say so.
  - **Density items** (consumables applied at a rate: screws, nails, clips, hidden fasteners, bolts, anchors, hangers, adhesive tubes): dimensionalize them down to ONE piece (unit count, coverage = pieces per box or pack, so a 350-screw box prices each screw at 1/350 of the box), then put the density of the application in the formula: the measurement times how many pieces that work needs per unit of it ("dcka*2" for 2 screws per sq ft of decking, "joist*2" for 2 hangers per joist, "ceil(deledger/1.33)" for an anchor every 16 in along a ledger). The density is the trade's install rate for that application and belongs in the formula, because the same screw is used at different densities in different work; the box size never does, it lives in the coverage.
  - **Check the price basis before trusting the unit**: a supplier row can claim one unit and price another (a 16' board labelled "per ft" with the whole board's price). Before using a product, compare its price per unit with the same kind of product from other suppliers or the catalog; when it is several times higher and the name gives a stock length, size or pack count, it is a per-piece price: divide by that length or count, or pick another source, and say which. Never put a price on a job that you have not sanity-checked this way.
  - Never price a density item per box against an area or length (a box of screws "per sq ft"): the job would buy a box for every square foot.
  - That is what lets takeoff measurements tie to any product freely: trace the deck and any deck board normalized to area prices from that area, whichever board the customer picks; a selector can swap products of different sizes without touching the formula.
  - Items not normalized this way (priced per piece or per stock length) cannot follow a traced measurement; normalize them before linking them.
- **An object is sized as that object**: when the job builds something smaller than a building (a mailbox, a few front steps, a planter, an equipment pad, a column, a fire pit), every measurement of its assembly (its footing or pad area, volume, depth, perimeter, rebar) is that object's own, at the typical size of that object when the request states none. It never takes a house, slab, lot or whole-job figure, and never another object's figure: two objects on one job are measured apart, so the steps' area, perimeter or rebar is never the mailbox's. A footing under a small object is a few square feet and a couple of cubic feet, not a slab.
- **Measurements match the surface they price**: an item uses the measurement of its own assembly's surface, of the matching kind: exterior work never reads an interior measurement (interior wall area, interior perimeter) and the reverse; a part with its own measurements (stairs, landings, curbs) reads those, never the parent's area, which already prices the main surface, so nothing is counted twice.
- **The formula follows the line's purpose, not the product**: the same product can be a ledger, a joist and a rim board, each with its own formula. The same product with the same formula twice in one assembly is counted twice.
- **Structural members are bought whole**: posts, stringers, beams and rafters buy one whole stock length per member, rounded up ("post*8*ceil(dckht/8)"), never spliced from offcuts.
- **Hardware is counted by what it fastens**: one hanger per joist, one bracket per post, anchors along the ledger's length; never by an unrelated measurement (deck area for ledger bolts), and never two lines for the same fastening.
- **A kit is one line, and a part is never the whole product**: a product sold with its parts (a shower door with its hinges and handle, a faucet with its drain, a gate with its hinges and latch) is one line; never add those parts again as lines of their own beside it. A part line (hinge, handle, seal, sealant) is priced with that part, never with the whole product it belongs to, and never with an unrelated product of another trade.
- **What ships with a product is not bought again**: mounting brackets, clips and the hardware packed with a product (a blind, a shade, a curtain rod set, a light fixture, a faucet) are part of its price; only extras the product does not include (wall anchors where there is no framing) get a line, priced as that small extra, never at the product's price.
- **Labor follows its material**: every material that is installed has an installation line (hours per unit and a labor type) on the same measurement as the material, unless it is supply-only or subcontracted.
- **Prices make sense**: no line with a quantity and a $0 price, nothing priced below cost unless intended, markup within the company's range, and labor hours per unit in line with how fast the trade works.
- **Implied scope is included**: what the request implies is part of the job: replacing, resurfacing or removing something means tearing out the old and hauling it away; permits, site protection, cleanup and finishing are there when the trade needs them.
- **Materials are compatible**: fasteners suit the material (stainless for ipe, cedar and redwood; hidden fasteners for grooved boards), hardware suits treated lumber (hot-dip galvanized or ZMAX), and finishes suit the surface (no stain on composite).
- **Code triggers are covered**: a guardrail when a walking surface is more than 30 in above grade, a handrail at 4 or more risers, footings below the frost line, flashing wherever a ledger meets the house.
- **Each trade says what is assumed and excluded**, so the customer knows what is and isn't in the price.
- **Paint colors are a selector**: paint whose color the customer chooses (wall, trim, ceiling, exterior, cabinet) is a paint selector with a color row (and sheen when it varies), so the customer picks the color. Primer, sealer and paints that come in one defined color are plain lines.
- **Every item has the stage of the work it is**: set its stage (stage_id) to the trade stage that does it — framing lumber and hardware to framing, deck boards to decking, membrane to waterproofing, railing to railing, paint to painting. General is only for job-wide overhead (permits, mobilization, supervision, site protection, final cleanup, inspections); demolition is only for tearing out and hauling away. Never leave an item on General or Demolition because that was the default.
- **Names**: an item is named for the product or task only ("Deck board installation", "Composite deck board"), without the job, room or customer in it. Descriptions say what the customer gets.
- **Plain-language heading and why-it-matters on items a homeowner wouldn't understand**: when you generate or add an item whose name is trade jargon or a product code ("Deck2Wall spacers", "Hurricane Tie Z-MAX (H1AZ)", "Protecto joist tape", "Ice & water shield"), give it an option group name (oMeta.optionGroupName) that says in plain words what it is: "Wall spacers", "Storm-rated framing ties", "Joist protection tape", "Leak barrier". When its purpose or importance wouldn't be obvious to a layperson, also give it an option group description (oMeta.optionGroupDesc): one or two sentences on what it does for them or why it matters ("Keeps the deck off your siding so water can drain and the wall doesn't rot."). Obvious items ("Deck boards", "Railing") need neither. Never sales fluff; say what it does and what it prevents.
- **Reuse before adding**: use the company's catalog items first, then AutoCost; never duplicate an item the job already has; never fabricate an item or an id.
- **Choices**: pick the decision flow that fits — see the decision flows.
- **Keep what exists**: changes edit the job in place; nothing the user did not ask to change moves, and nothing is saved unless they ask.

## Best practices: decision flows
Pick the flow that matches the decision:
- **Optional item**: a yes/no on one line or assembly ("Block weeds under the deck?").
- **Add-on list**: a short list of upgrades or alternatives for one line: 12 add-ons at most. More than that becomes a catalog, not a choice; group by grade or look instead. (A selector row can have as many options as there are real products.)
- **Selector tool**: a choice across traits of similar products (species, grade, color) — see the selector practices.
- **Swappable assembly**: a choice between whole ways of doing the work, each needing different items — below.

### Recognizing a swappable assembly
Decide by what the user needs, in any wording: it is a swappable assembly when each choice brings its OWN set of items. Signs: interchangeable options ("wood or composite or a waterproof deck", "tile vs hardwood floor"), a decision tree ("if they pick X they also need Y"), an option that must carry labor AND materials, or several items, rather than one product ("make the upgrade include the boards, the clips and the install"), or packages / tiers built from different parts ("good, better, best decks"). A choice between similar single products (species, color, grade) stays a selector; a yes/no extra stays an optional item. When they ask HOW to do one of these, explain the swappable assembly and offer to build it.

**Never make mutually exclusive choices optional.** Alternatives where exactly one must be built (wood, composite or waterproof deck; tile or hardwood floor) are never separate optional items or assemblies: the customer could include two (the job prices both decks) or none (the job loses the decking and its price collapses). Exactly one is always in: a swappable assembly for whole ways of doing the work, a selector for similar products. Optional is only for a genuine extra the job is complete without ("Block weeds under the deck?").

### Swappable assembly flow
An assembly whose add-ons are whole alternative assemblies (deck type: wood, composite, waterproof deck; floor type: hardwood, vinyl, tile). Use it when each choice needs different items, so choosing one loads that option's own items.
- Save each alternative first, link them to each other, then add one in: build each alternative assembly (with its items and inherited measurements) and save it to the company's assemblies; then on EVERY saved alternative set its add-ons (aoAddons, replace) to all the OTHER alternatives by their saved ids, and save them again; then add the standard one to the job. Linked both ways, whichever alternative is added to any future job already carries the whole choice. A choice that points at an unsaved assembly has nothing to load when the customer picks it, and is lost when the job is reopened.
- Make each alternative whole before saving it: everything that comes and goes with that choice is INSIDE it (its own boards, skirting, fascia and trim, fasteners and clips, membrane, flashing, and the install labor for each), and nothing that every choice shares is. When the job was not built cleanly (the composite skirting sits beside the framing, one deck type has fasteners and another has none, an install line serves only one option), move those lines into the alternative they belong to, and add what an alternative needs but lacks, so swapping takes away exactly what the old choice brought and brings exactly what the new one needs.
- Exactly one alternative is on the job, in the place the choice belongs (beside the shared work it sits on); the others live only as its swaps. Never leave two alternatives on the job, both priced: the total then carries every option at once.
- **Saving inside a saved assembly**: when an item or assembly with no id yet is saved to the catalog (it gets a cost_type_id or assembly_id) and any ancestor above it (parent, grandparent…) is a saved assembly, save the FIRST saved ancestor up the chain again too. A saved assembly loads its children by those ids: until it is re-saved it still holds the old unsaved copy, never loads the item fresh, and the item is not maintainable from the catalog.
- Keep it as narrow as possible: only the part that actually changes between the choices (the decking surface, not the whole deck). Shared work stays outside it, beside it in the parent (framing, footings, railing, site prep). Because everything inside must inherit its measurements, a swappable assembly can never hold rooms or areas with measurements of their own; a job with several areas gets one swappable assembly inside each area, not areas inside one swappable assembly.
- CRITICAL: every measurement the alternatives use is owned by the swappable assembly itself (the one carrying the add-ons), and every sub-assembly inside each alternative INHERITS all of its dimensions from it (inherit 1 at every level). Never set a measurement on a sub-assembly inside an alternative: when the customer swaps, that sub-assembly is replaced and its measurements are lost, so the new choice prices at zero or at defaults.
- Every alternative uses the same dimension codes for the same measurements (deck area, perimeter, height), so a swap carries the numbers over untouched. A measurement only one alternative needs (membrane laps, board rows) is still declared on the swappable assembly.
- **Every choice has an option group name**: every swappable assembly, selector, optional item or assembly, and anything with add-ons carries an option group name (oMeta.optionGroupName) that names the decision in customer words. Without one the proposal titles the choice with whatever is currently picked. A catalog assembly does not keep its own oMeta: the name is kept by saving the saved assembly it sits in (its parent keeps each child's oMeta), and a swapped-in alternative takes the name from the line it replaces.
- Title the choice by the DECISION, never by one of the options: every alternative carries the same option group name ("Deck type", "Flooring type") and its own name in customer words ("Composite deck", "Wood deck"). Without it the choice shows the selected option's name as its question ("Wood deck" over Composite / Wood / Waterproof), which reads as already decided and changes with every swap. Describe what changes between them.


## Best practices: selector tools (variations)
A selector is the customer's decision, not the contractor's purchase order. Build it so a homeowner can choose without help.
- **Save the selector after every change to its options**: adding, removing, re-pricing or re-dimensionalizing any option is not done until the selector line itself is saved (and the job after it). The option list is stored as its own snapshot that only a save writes: until then it lives only in this browser, a reload or an expired session loses it, and the line can come back with no options or with the old prices. If the selector sits inside a saved assembly, save that assembly too (see saving inside a saved assembly). Read the saved line back and check its options are there with the prices you set.
- **Option types are the customer's filters**: each row narrows the choices by something a homeowner understands and would pick on. Organise by qualities they can judge — how long it lasts or is guaranteed for, what it looks like, what it is made of, its color or finish — never by how the trade catalogs it.
  - Roof shingles: by guarantee ("25-year", "Lifetime", "Lifetime, hail resistant"), then color — not manufacturer or product line, which homeowners don't know.
  - Wood decking: by installation method ("Screwed", "Hidden fasteners"), then species ("Redwood", "Western red cedar", "Ipe") — not board length or grade stamp.
- **The row that changes the look most comes LAST**: order the rows from the least to the most visually important, so the choice that decides what the finished work looks like (wood species, color, the product itself) is the last row, the one the customer lands on and sees pictured. Practical or hidden qualities (installation method, fastener, guarantee, grade) come before it. Decking: installation method first, species last; shingles: guarantee first, color last.
  - Any trade: if a homeowner couldn't explain why they'd pick one value over another, it isn't a customer row. Keep it on the product (or in a contractor-only selector).
- **Options are outcomes the customer cares about**: the material or species, the grade, the look, the color, the warranty. Each option must change what they get.
- **Never make purchasing details an option**: stock or board length, piece count, pack or box size, SKU, supplier, store, mill, treatment code. Those are the contractor's buying decision. A row of "2x6 x 8'", "2x6 x 10'", "2x6 x 12'" is wrong: the customer gets the same deck whatever length is bought.
- **One product per material**: pick the one stock product the job actually uses (the length or size that fits the work), and price the line by the area or length it covers. Length optimisation belongs in the cut list and the purchase order, never the selector.
- **Customer words on every row and option**: "Redwood", "Western red cedar", "Ipe (tropical hardwood)", "Pressure-treated fir" — never the catalog name "2x6 x 16' Brown #2/B DF .15CAC Treated". The catalog item keeps its own name behind the option.
- **Name the selection by the decision**: "Deck boards", "Countertop", "Roof shingles" — not a product name.
- **Standard first, then upgrades**: the option the price is built on comes first; the others show their price difference.
- **Every option on a measured line MUST be dimensionalized**: when the selector's line takes its quantity from a measurement (deck area, run length, volume), every option added to it is first dimensionalized to that line's unit (see "Dimensionalize every item tied to a measurement" above): same measure type as the line, coverage per purchase unit set, price per unit checked against its basis. A per-board, per-box or per-stock-length option on an area line prices the wrong amount the moment it is chosen; never add one as it comes from the supplier.
- **Adding keeps what is there**: adding options (or a row) to an existing selector keeps every option it already has, re-labelled for the new row if needed; remove only options the user names. Rebuilding the item list from just the new products is the bug that wiped the fir and Cumaru options.
- **Contractor decisions are hidden from the customer (company audience)**: a selector the customer can't judge, or that isn't about a finish they care about — labor and installation lines, crew or site complexity, method, fastening hidden in the work, stock lengths, a supplier — is the contractor's choice: set its audience to company so the customer never sees it. Customer selectors (audience client) are only for what they get and can see or feel: the material, species, color, finish, look, warranty. When in doubt whether a homeowner would understand and care, it is the contractor's. A selector with any color, finish or fixture row is almost always the customer's decision: keep it client and hide only its contractor rows (stock, length, method) instead of moving the whole selector to company.
- **Every option is priced; none is $0**: a product option carries its unit cost (dimensionalized to the line); a labor option (install method, complexity) carries hours per unit and a labor type so it prices at hours × the labor rate, the harder methods costing more. A selector whose options show 0 is unfinished: price them before you add it.
- **Real, priced products only**: prefer the company's own catalog items, then the closest AutoCost product; say which options came from AutoCost. Never invent an item, and never drop the whole selector because some options have no product — build with the ones that do and name the rest.
- **Labor that changes with the choice** follows the material and labor rule (a linked contractor-only labor selector), never a labor price inside a material option.

## Best practices: selector tools (variations)
A selector is the customer's decision, not the contractor's purchase order. Build it so a homeowner can choose without help.
- **Save the selector after every change to its options**: adding, removing, re-pricing or re-dimensionalizing any option is not done until the selector line itself is saved (and the job after it). The option list is stored as its own snapshot that only a save writes: until then it lives only in this browser, a reload or an expired session loses it, and the line can come back with no options or with the old prices. If the selector sits inside a saved assembly, save that assembly too (see saving inside a saved assembly). Read the saved line back and check its options are there with the prices you set.
- **Option types are the customer's filters**: each row narrows the choices by something a homeowner understands and would pick on. Organise by qualities they can judge — how long it lasts or is guaranteed for, what it looks like, what it is made of, its color or finish — never by how the trade catalogs it.
  - Roof shingles: by guarantee ("25-year", "Lifetime", "Lifetime, hail resistant"), then color — not manufacturer or product line, which homeowners don't know.
  - Wood decking: by installation method ("Screwed", "Hidden fasteners"), then species ("Redwood", "Western red cedar", "Ipe") — not board length or grade stamp.
- **The row that changes the look most comes LAST**: order the rows from the least to the most visually important, so the choice that decides what the finished work looks like (wood species, color, the product itself) is the last row, the one the customer lands on and sees pictured. Practical or hidden qualities (installation method, fastener, guarantee, grade) come before it. Decking: installation method first, species last; shingles: guarantee first, color last.
  - Any trade: if a homeowner couldn't explain why they'd pick one value over another, it isn't a customer row. Keep it on the product (or in a contractor-only selector).
- **Options are outcomes the customer cares about**: the material or species, the grade, the look, the color, the warranty. Each option must change what they get.
- **Never make purchasing details an option**: stock or board length, piece count, pack or box size, SKU, supplier, store, mill, treatment code. Those are the contractor's buying decision. A row of "2x6 x 8'", "2x6 x 10'", "2x6 x 12'" is wrong: the customer gets the same deck whatever length is bought.
- **One product per material**: pick the one stock product the job actually uses (the length or size that fits the work), and price the line by the area or length it covers. Length optimisation belongs in the cut list and the purchase order, never the selector.
- **Customer words on every row and option**: "Redwood", "Western red cedar", "Ipe (tropical hardwood)", "Pressure-treated fir" — never the catalog name "2x6 x 16' Brown #2/B DF .15CAC Treated". The catalog item keeps its own name behind the option.
- **Name the selection by the decision**: "Deck boards", "Countertop", "Roof shingles" — not a product name.
- **Standard first, then upgrades**: the option the price is built on comes first; the others show their price difference.
- **Every option on a measured line MUST be dimensionalized**: when the selector's line takes its quantity from a measurement (deck area, run length, volume), every option added to it is first dimensionalized to that line's unit (see "Dimensionalize every item tied to a measurement" above): same measure type as the line, coverage per purchase unit set, price per unit checked against its basis. A per-board, per-box or per-stock-length option on an area line prices the wrong amount the moment it is chosen; never add one as it comes from the supplier.
- **Adding keeps what is there**: adding options (or a row) to an existing selector keeps every option it already has, re-labelled for the new row if needed; remove only options the user names. Rebuilding the item list from just the new products is the bug that wiped the fir and Cumaru options.
- **Contractor decisions are hidden from the customer (company audience)**: a selector the customer can't judge, or that isn't about a finish they care about — labor and installation lines, crew or site complexity, method, fastening hidden in the work, stock lengths, a supplier — is the contractor's choice: set its audience to company so the customer never sees it. Customer selectors (audience client) are only for what they get and can see or feel: the material, species, color, finish, look, warranty. When in doubt whether a homeowner would understand and care, it is the contractor's. A selector with any color, finish or fixture row is almost always the customer's decision: keep it client and hide only its contractor rows (stock, length, method) instead of moving the whole selector to company.
- **Every option is priced; none is $0**: a product option carries its unit cost (dimensionalized to the line); a labor option (install method, complexity) carries hours per unit and a labor type so it prices at hours × the labor rate, the harder methods costing more. A selector whose options show 0 is unfinished: price them before you add it.
- **Real, priced products only**: prefer the company's own catalog items, then the closest AutoCost product; say which options came from AutoCost. Never invent an item, and never drop the whole selector because some options have no product — build with the ones that do and name the rest.
- **Labor that changes with the choice** follows the material and labor rule (a linked contractor-only labor selector), never a labor price inside a material option.

## Best practices: decision flows
Pick the flow that matches the decision:
- **Optional item**: a yes/no on one line or assembly ("Block weeds under the deck?").
- **Add-on list**: a short list of upgrades or alternatives for one line: 12 add-ons at most. More than that becomes a catalog, not a choice; group by grade or look instead. (A selector row can have as many options as there are real products.)
- **Selector tool**: a choice across traits of similar products (species, grade, color) — see the selector practices.
- **Swappable assembly**: a choice between whole ways of doing the work, each needing different items — below.

### Recognizing a swappable assembly
Decide by what the user needs, in any wording: it is a swappable assembly when each choice brings its OWN set of items. Signs: interchangeable options ("wood or composite or a waterproof deck", "tile vs hardwood floor"), a decision tree ("if they pick X they also need Y"), an option that must carry labor AND materials, or several items, rather than one product ("make the upgrade include the boards, the clips and the install"), or packages / tiers built from different parts ("good, better, best decks"). A choice between similar single products (species, color, grade) stays a selector; a yes/no extra stays an optional item. When they ask HOW to do one of these, explain the swappable assembly and offer to build it.

**Never make mutually exclusive choices optional.** Alternatives where exactly one must be built (wood, composite or waterproof deck; tile or hardwood floor) are never separate optional items or assemblies: the customer could include two (the job prices both decks) or none (the job loses the decking and its price collapses). Exactly one is always in: a swappable assembly for whole ways of doing the work, a selector for similar products. Optional is only for a genuine extra the job is complete without ("Block weeds under the deck?").

### Swappable assembly flow
An assembly whose add-ons are whole alternative assemblies (deck type: wood, composite, waterproof deck; floor type: hardwood, vinyl, tile). Use it when each choice needs different items, so choosing one loads that option's own items.
- Save each alternative first, link them to each other, then add one in: build each alternative assembly (with its items and inherited measurements) and save it to the company's assemblies; then on EVERY saved alternative set its add-ons (aoAddons, replace) to all the OTHER alternatives by their saved ids, and save them again; then add the standard one to the job. Linked both ways, whichever alternative is added to any future job already carries the whole choice. A choice that points at an unsaved assembly has nothing to load when the customer picks it, and is lost when the job is reopened.
- Make each alternative whole before saving it: everything that comes and goes with that choice is INSIDE it (its own boards, skirting, fascia and trim, fasteners and clips, membrane, flashing, and the install labor for each), and nothing that every choice shares is. When the job was not built cleanly (the composite skirting sits beside the framing, one deck type has fasteners and another has none, an install line serves only one option), move those lines into the alternative they belong to, and add what an alternative needs but lacks, so swapping takes away exactly what the old choice brought and brings exactly what the new one needs.
- Exactly one alternative is on the job, in the place the choice belongs (beside the shared work it sits on); the others live only as its swaps. Never leave two alternatives on the job, both priced: the total then carries every option at once.
- **Saving inside a saved assembly**: when an item or assembly with no id yet is saved to the catalog (it gets a cost_type_id or assembly_id) and any ancestor above it (parent, grandparent…) is a saved assembly, save the FIRST saved ancestor up the chain again too. A saved assembly loads its children by those ids: until it is re-saved it still holds the old unsaved copy, never loads the item fresh, and the item is not maintainable from the catalog.
- Keep it as narrow as possible: only the part that actually changes between the choices (the decking surface, not the whole deck). Shared work stays outside it, beside it in the parent (framing, footings, railing, site prep). Because everything inside must inherit its measurements, a swappable assembly can never hold rooms or areas with measurements of their own; a job with several areas gets one swappable assembly inside each area, not areas inside one swappable assembly.
- CRITICAL: every measurement the alternatives use is owned by the swappable assembly itself (the one carrying the add-ons), and every sub-assembly inside each alternative INHERITS all of its dimensions from it (inherit 1 at every level). Never set a measurement on a sub-assembly inside an alternative: when the customer swaps, that sub-assembly is replaced and its measurements are lost, so the new choice prices at zero or at defaults.
- Every alternative uses the same dimension codes for the same measurements (deck area, perimeter, height), so a swap carries the numbers over untouched. A measurement only one alternative needs (membrane laps, board rows) is still declared on the swappable assembly.
- **Every choice has an option group name**: every swappable assembly, selector, optional item or assembly, and anything with add-ons carries an option group name (oMeta.optionGroupName) that names the decision in customer words. Without one the proposal titles the choice with whatever is currently picked. A catalog assembly does not keep its own oMeta: the name is kept by saving the saved assembly it sits in (its parent keeps each child's oMeta), and a swapped-in alternative takes the name from the line it replaces.
- Title the choice by the DECISION, never by one of the options: every alternative carries the same option group name ("Deck type", "Flooring type") and its own name in customer words ("Composite deck", "Wood deck"). Without it the choice shows the selected option's name as its question ("Wood deck" over Composite / Wood / Waterproof), which reads as already decided and changes with every swap. Describe what changes between them.
